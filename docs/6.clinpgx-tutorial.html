ClinPGx | From genotype to prescription

A pharmacogenomics walk-through using CYP2C19 and clopidogrel as an example

Author
Affiliation

Published

October 2026

Abstract

A hands-on introduction to ClinPGx (the successor of PharmGKB), the reference knowledgebase for pharmacogenomics. Following one gene (CYP2C19) and one drug (clopidogrel), you will learn the core concepts of pharmacogenomics (star alleles, diplotypes, metabolizer phenotypes), read a CPIC dosing guideline, interpret drug labels and levels of evidence, and connect ClinPGx to Ensembl, UniProt, PDB and DrugBank.

ImportantDisclaimer

This lesson is for education only. The genotypes and patients used here are fictional. Real prescribing decisions must follow the current version of the guidelines, the approved drug label, and the judgement of the clinical team.

1 Why is ClinPGx relevant for pharmaceutical sciences?

Two patients receive the same drug at the same dose. One responds well, the other has no benefit, and a third may have a serious adverse reaction. Part of this variability is genetic. Pharmacogenomics (PGx) studies how inherited variation in our genes changes the way we respond to medicines.

In previous classes you followed one entity at a time: a gene in Ensembl, a protein in UniProt, a structure in PDB, a drug in DrugBank. ClinPGx sits on top of all of them and answers a clinical question:

“Given this patient’s genotype, should I change the drug or the dose?”

That is a question pharmacists will increasingly be asked to answer, which is why this is our final database.

2 About ClinPGx

ClinPGx is a free resource, managed at Stanford University, that brings three projects under one roof:

The three components of ClinPGx
Project What it is What you get from it
PharmGKB The Pharmacogenomics Knowledgebase, curating PGx literature since 2000 Annotations of papers, drug labels and pathways
CPIC The Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium Peer-reviewed prescribing guidelines
PharmCAT The Pharmacogenomics Clinical Annotation Tool Software that turns a sequencing file (VCF) into a PGx report

PharmGKB was renamed ClinPGx in July 2025, and old pharmgkb.org links now redirect to the new site. In older papers you will still see the name PharmGKB: it is the same knowledgebase. One renaming to remember: what PharmGKB called clinical annotations are now called summary annotations.

3 Key concepts before we start

You only need six ideas to use ClinPGx well. Read them now; you will use each one during the session.

Note📘 Concept 1: Pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic genes
  • Pharmacokinetic (PK) genes change what the body does to the drug: absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion. Most are enzymes (e.g. the cytochromes P450, CYP2C19, CYP2D6) or transporters (e.g. SLCO1B1).
  • Pharmacodynamic (PD) genes change what the drug does to the body: the target (e.g. VKORC1 for warfarin) or the immune response (e.g. HLA-B and severe skin reactions).
Note📘 Concept 2: Star alleles and haplotypes

A gene can carry several variants at once. The combination of variants found together on one copy of a chromosome is a haplotype. In pharmacogenes, haplotypes are named with star (*) alleles: CYP2C19*1, CYP2C19*2, CYP2C19*17, and so on.

  • *1 is usually the reference (normal) allele.
  • Each other star allele is defined by one or more core variants, often identified by their dbSNP rsID (e.g. rs4244285).
  • The official naming authority for star alleles is PharmVar.
Note📘 Concept 3: Diplotype

We have two copies of each autosomal gene (one from each parent). The pair of star alleles a person carries is their diplotype, written CYP2C19*1/*2. A diplotype is the PGx equivalent of a genotype.

Note📘 Concept 4: Allele function and phenotype

Each star allele is assigned a function: normal, decreased, no function, or increased function. Combining the two alleles of a diplotype predicts a phenotype. For drug-metabolising enzymes the phenotypes are:

Standard metabolizer phenotypes
Phenotype Abbreviation Enzyme activity
Ultrarapid metabolizer UM Much higher than normal
Rapid metabolizer RM Higher than normal
Normal metabolizer NM Normal
Intermediate metabolizer IM Reduced
Poor metabolizer PM Little or none

How do two alleles give one phenotype? Think of each allele as contributing some enzyme activity, and add the two contributions together. For CYP2C19, the rules used by CPIC are:

CYP2C19: from allele functions to phenotype (simplified from CPIC)
Allele 1 Allele 2 Phenotype
increased increased Ultrarapid (UM)
normal increased Rapid (RM)
normal normal Normal (NM)
normal or increased no function Intermediate (IM)
no function no function Poor (PM)

Notice the last two rows: one no function allele is enough to reduce activity, even when the other allele has increased function. These rules are gene-specific, which is why each CPIC guideline has its own genotype-to-phenotype table.

Note📘 Concept 5: Prodrugs turn the logic upside down

For most drugs, a poor metabolizer clears the drug slowly, so drug levels rise and the risk of toxicity increases. But a prodrug is inactive until an enzyme converts it into the active form. For a prodrug, a poor metabolizer makes less active drug, so the risk is lack of efficacy.

Clopidogrel, our example today, is a prodrug activated mainly by CYP2C19.

  2. Open the gene-drug pairs list (https://www.clinpgx.org/pairs). What is it built from?

5 The gene page: CYP2C19

As in UniProt, the best way to find an entry is a unique identifier, here the official HGNC gene symbol.

Tip🔬 Hands on: open the CYP2C19 page
  1. Type CYP2C19 in the search bar.
  2. Select the gene result (not a drug or a variant).
  3. Note the identifier in the URL. It starts with PA (e.g. PA124).
  4. Browse the tabs of the gene page.

Every ClinPGx object has a stable PA identifier (a legacy of PharmGKB), in the same way that UniProt has accessions like P04000 and DrugBank has identifiers like DB00758.

Warning❓ Question
  1. Is CYP2C19 a pharmacokinetic or a pharmacodynamic gene? Why?
  2. CYP2C19 is labelled a VIP. What does that mean?
  3. List three drugs (or drug classes) for which a CPIC guideline exists for CYP2C19.
  4. In the cross-references, find the identifiers for Ensembl and UniProt. Do you recognise them from earlier classes?
NoteShow answer
  1. Pharmacokinetic: CYP2C19 is a liver enzyme that metabolises drugs.
  2. Very Important Pharmacogene: a gene with strong, well documented pharmacogenomic relevance, for which ClinPGx curators write a dedicated summary.
  3. Among others: clopidogrel, proton pump inhibitors (e.g. omeprazole, pantoprazole), voriconazole, some antidepressants (e.g. citalopram, escitalopram, sertraline) and tricyclic antidepressants.
  4. Ensembl ENSG00000165841 (chromosome 10) and UniProt P33261 (CP2CJ_HUMAN). The same gene and protein you could have studied in previous classes, now viewed from the clinical side.
Tip🔗 Connection: UniProt and PDB

Open the UniProt entry P33261 in a new tab and check the Function and Subcellular location sections. Then go to the Structure section, or search PDB for 4GQS, a crystal structure of human CYP2C19.

You now know where the enzyme is (endoplasmic reticulum membrane of liver cells) and what it looks like (a heme-containing P450 fold). ClinPGx tells you what happens to patients when its gene varies.

6.2 The variant behind CYP2C19*2

Tip🔬 Hands on: one variant, two databases
  1. In ClinPGx, search for rs4244285 and open the variant page.
  2. In a new tab, open the same variant in Ensembl: https://www.ensembl.org/Homo_sapiens/Variation/Explore?v=rs4244285.
  3. In Ensembl, look at the predicted consequence of the variant on the transcript, and at its Allele frequency tab.
Warning❓ Question
  1. Which star allele does rs4244285 define?
  2. What consequence does Ensembl predict for the protein sequence?
  3. If the amino acid does not change, how can this be a no function allele? (Hint: read carefully the top Description in the Overview section of the variant rs4244285.)`
NoteShow answer
  1. CYP2C19*2 (c.681G>A).
  2. Ensembl reports it as a synonymous variant (the codon still encodes proline 227).
  3. The change creates an aberrant splice site. The mRNA is spliced incorrectly, the reading frame shifts, and a truncated, non-functional enzyme is produced. This is a classic lesson: “synonymous” does not mean “harmless”. Automatic predictors look at the codon; curated databases like ClinPGx integrate experimental evidence.
NoteKnowledge keeps moving

In August 2026, PharmVar updated the core variant definition of CYP2C19*2. As with the UniProt History tab, the definition of an allele is a moving target, and ClinPGx and PharmCAT follow these updates. See the ClinPGx blog post CYP2C19*2 core allele definition update: https://blog.clinpgx.org/cyp2c19-2-core-allele-definition-update/.

6.3 Allele frequency across populations

Tip🔬 Hands on: allele frequencies
  1. Open the allele frequency pages: https://www.clinpgx.org/alleleFrequency.
  2. Select CYP2C19 and compare the frequency of *2, *3 and *17 across the biogeographical groups (e.g. European, East Asian, Sub-Saharan African).
Warning❓ Question
  1. In which group is *2 most frequent? And *17?
  2. *3 is almost absent in Europeans. Where is it found?
  3. Compare the *2 frequency in ClinPGx with the frequency of the A allele of rs4244285 in Ensembl (1000 Genomes populations). Are they similar? Why might they differ slightly?
  4. Why does this matter for a country’s decision to implement genotyping?
NoteShow answer
  1. *2 is most frequent in East Asian and Oceanian populations (roughly 30% or more, versus roughly 15% in Europeans). *17 is common in Europeans and Africans (around 20%) but rare in East Asians.
  2. Mainly in East Asian populations (a few percent).
  3. They should be close. Differences come from different source studies, sample sizes, population definitions, and from the fact that a star allele is a haplotype while an rsID is a single variant.
  4. The proportion of patients who would change treatment after testing depends on allele frequencies. In populations where poor and intermediate metabolizers are common, testing changes more prescriptions.

7 The drug page and the CPIC guideline

Tip🔬 Hands on: open the clopidogrel page
  1. Search for clopidogrel and open the chemical page.
  2. Look at the cross-references and find the DrugBank identifier.
  3. Find the Prescribing information or Guidelines area and open the CPIC guideline for CYP2C19 and clopidogrel.
Warning❓ Question
  1. What is the DrugBank identifier for clopidogrel?
  2. What is clopidogrel used for, and what is its mechanism of action?
  3. Why is CYP2C19 so important for this particular drug?
NoteShow answer
  1. DB00758.
  2. It is an antiplatelet agent used to prevent heart attacks and strokes, particularly after acute coronary syndrome and after stent placement (PCI). Its active metabolite irreversibly blocks the platelet P2Y12 receptor.
  3. Clopidogrel is a prodrug. CYP2C19 performs most of its conversion to the active metabolite. Poor and intermediate metabolizers make less active drug, so their platelets are less inhibited and they have a higher risk of cardiovascular events.
Tip🔗 Connection: DrugBank

Open DB00758 in DrugBank. Look at Enzymes (under Targets) and at the Pharmacogenomic Effects/ADRs and Drug Interactions sections.

  • DrugBank tells you which enzymes handle the drug and which drugs interact with it.
  • ClinPGx tells you what to do when the patient’s genes change how those enzymes work.

7.1 Reading the CPIC recommendations

A CPIC guideline does not tell you whether to genotype a patient. It assumes the genotype is already known and tells you how to use it. Each recommendation has a strength: strong, moderate, or optional.

Tip🔬 Hands on: the recommendation table
  1. On the guideline page, find the recommendation table (or the interactive tool that lets you pick a phenotype).
  2. Select the cardiovascular indication (acute coronary syndrome and/or PCI).
  3. Read the recommendation for each CYP2C19 phenotype.
Warning❓ Question
  1. What does CPIC recommend for a poor metabolizer? With what strength?
  2. And for an intermediate metabolizer?
  3. What about normal, rapid and ultrarapid metabolizers?
  4. Why not simply give a poor metabolizer a higher dose of clopidogrel?
NoteShow answer

Based on the 2022 CPIC update (cardiovascular indications):

  1. Avoid clopidogrel if possible, and use prasugrel or ticagrelor at standard dose if there is no contraindication. Strong recommendation.
  2. Also avoid standard-dose clopidogrel if possible, and use prasugrel or ticagrelor if there is no contraindication. Moderate strength (strengthened compared with the 2013 guideline).
  3. Use clopidogrel at the standard dose. The increased function of *17 does not justify a change.
  4. In poor metabolizers, even higher doses do not produce enough active metabolite to reach adequate platelet inhibition. Prasugrel and ticagrelor do not depend on CYP2C19 in the same way, so they are the logical alternatives.
Note📘 Concept 7: CPIC levels for gene-drug pairs

Before writing a guideline, CPIC assigns each gene-drug pair a level:

CPIC levels (simplified)
CPIC level Meaning
A Genetic information should be used to change prescribing
B Genetic information could be used to change prescribing
C No prescribing action recommended (evidence or alternatives insufficient)
D No prescribing action recommended

Only levels A and B lead to prescribing guidelines. CYP2C19 and clopidogrel is level A. You can check the level of any pair in the gene-drug pairs list.

8 Drug labels

Regulatory agencies (FDA in the USA, EMA in Europe, PMDA in Japan, Swissmedic, Health Canada) sometimes include pharmacogenomic information in the official drug label. ClinPGx curates these labels and classifies them:

Pharmacogenomic levels of drug labels
ClinPGx label level Meaning
Testing required The label says genetic testing must be done before prescribing
Testing recommended The label recommends testing
Actionable PGx The label suggests a change (drug, dose) based on genotype, without requiring testing
Informative PGx The label mentions a gene-drug effect with no clinical action
Tip🔬 Hands on: clopidogrel labels
  1. On the clopidogrel page, open the drug label annotations.
  2. Compare the FDA label with the EMA label.
Warning❓ Question
  1. Which agencies have a label annotation for clopidogrel?
  2. Which PGx level is given to the FDA label? Does it require testing?
  3. The FDA label carries a boxed warning. What is it about?
  4. Search for abacavir and look at its labels. How does its level compare with clopidogrel? Why?
NoteShow answer
  1. Several, including the FDA, EMA, PMDA, Swissmedic and Health Canada (the exact list may vary).
  2. Actionable PGx (at the time of writing). Testing is not required, but the label warns about reduced effectiveness in CYP2C19 poor metabolizers and advises considering another P2Y12 inhibitor.
  3. Diminished antiplatelet effect in patients who are CYP2C19 poor metabolizers.
  4. Abacavir labels are Testing required: patients must be tested for HLA-B*57:01 before starting, because carriers are at high risk of a severe, potentially fatal hypersensitivity reaction. Here the consequence (life-threatening toxicity) and the evidence are strong enough to make testing mandatory.

9 Summary annotations and levels of evidence

Guidelines and labels exist for a few hundred gene-drug pairs. For thousands of other associations, ClinPGx offers summary annotations: a curated summary of all papers on one variant-drug pair, graded with a level of evidence computed from a scoring system (study size, replication, p-values, existence of guidelines, and so on).

ClinPGx levels of evidence for summary annotations
Level Evidence In short
1A High Variant-specific prescribing guidance in a guideline or FDA-approved label
1B High Strong, replicated association, but no variant-specific guidance yet
2A Moderate Moderate evidence, in a well-known pharmacogene (VIP)
2B Moderate Moderate evidence, outside the VIPs
3 Low Single study, not replicated, or conflicting results
4 Unsupported The evidence does not support an association
Tip🔬 Hands on: grading the evidence
  1. Open the summary annotations: https://www.clinpgx.org/summaryAnnotations (or the Summary annotations section of the clopidogrel page).
  2. Filter for rs4244285 and clopidogrel.
  3. Open the annotation and read the text given for each genotype (GG, GA, AA).
  4. Scroll down to the list of supporting variant annotations (the individual papers).
Warning❓ Question
  1. What is the level of evidence of rs4244285 and clopidogrel? Why so high?
  2. What phenotype categories are covered (e.g. efficacy, toxicity, metabolism/PK)?
  3. Do all supporting papers agree? What does this tell you about single studies?
  4. Find a summary annotation for clopidogrel with level 3. Would you change a prescription based on it?
NoteShow answer
  1. Level 1A: the variant is part of a CPIC guideline and FDA label with variant-specific prescribing guidance, and it is supported by many independent studies.
  2. Typically efficacy (e.g. cardiovascular events, platelet reactivity) and metabolism/PK (active metabolite exposure).
  3. No. Some studies show an association, others do not (different populations, sample sizes, outcomes, co-medications). This is why evidence is summarised and graded instead of relying on any single paper.
  4. No. Level 3 evidence is preliminary. It is interesting for research, but it is not a basis for clinical decisions.
Warning❓ Critical thinking question

A colleague finds a paper reporting that a variant in gene X doubles the risk of a side effect of drug Y. In ClinPGx the corresponding summary annotation is level 3. Your colleague wants to recommend genotyping for all patients starting drug Y.

What would you answer? Which additional information would you look for in ClinPGx before deciding?

10 Putting it together: a patient case

Tip🔬 Hands on: from genotype to prescription

Mr. A., 62 years old, has just had a coronary stent implanted (PCI) after an acute coronary syndrome. The cardiologist plans to prescribe clopidogrel. A pharmacogenomic test reports: CYP2C19 *1/*2. He also takes omeprazole for gastric protection.

  1. Translate the diplotype into a phenotype (Section Section 6).
  2. Find the CPIC recommendation (Section Section 7.1).
  3. Open PharmDOG, the ClinPGx prescribing tool. Enter the CYP2C19 result and the drug clopidogrel, and compare its output with your answer.
  4. In DrugBank, check the interaction between clopidogrel and omeprazole.
Warning❓ Question
  1. What is Mr. A.’s phenotype, and what do you recommend?
  2. Why does the omeprazole matter?
  3. What is phenoconversion?
NoteShow answer
  1. *1/*2 is an intermediate metabolizer. CPIC recommends avoiding standard-dose clopidogrel if possible and using prasugrel or ticagrelor if there is no contraindication (moderate recommendation).
  2. Omeprazole inhibits CYP2C19. It can further reduce clopidogrel activation, and regulatory labels advise avoiding the combination with omeprazole or esomeprazole. A different gastroprotective strategy (or a different antiplatelet) should be discussed.
  3. Phenoconversion is when a co-administered drug (an inhibitor or inducer) makes the observed phenotype different from the one predicted by the genotype. A genetic normal metabolizer taking a strong inhibitor may behave like an intermediate or poor metabolizer. Genes are not the only factor: always check the whole medication list. PharmDOG can account for phenoconversion for some genes (e.g. CYP2D6).

Warning❓ Challenge (if time allows)

Repeat the reasoning chain for CYP2D6 and codeine.

  1. Is codeine a prodrug? What is it converted into?
  2. What does CPIC recommend for a CYP2D6 ultrarapid metabolizer? And for a poor metabolizer?
  3. Why is the risk for ultrarapid metabolizers particularly serious?
NoteShow answer
  1. Yes. CYP2D6 converts codeine into morphine, the active opioid.
  2. For both ultrarapid and poor metabolizers, CPIC recommends avoiding codeine and using a non-tramadol alternative. For ultrarapid metabolizers the risk is toxicity; for poor metabolizers it is lack of pain relief.
  3. Ultrarapid metabolizers produce morphine quickly and in large amounts, which can cause life-threatening respiratory depression. Deaths have been reported in children after tonsillectomy and in breastfed infants of ultrarapid metabolizer mothers. Note the symmetry with clopidogrel: the same logic (prodrug activation) leads to opposite risks at the two ends of the phenotype scale.

Key points

TipTake home messages
  • ClinPGx (formerly PharmGKB) integrates PharmGKB, CPIC and PharmCAT into one free pharmacogenomics resource.
  • The core reasoning is genotype → diplotype → phenotype → recommendation.
  • Star alleles name haplotypes; each has a function, and two alleles together predict a metabolizer phenotype.
  • For prodrugs such as clopidogrel and codeine, low enzyme activity means less effect, not more toxicity.
  • CPIC guidelines tell you how to use a known genotype; drug labels show the regulatory position; levels of evidence tell you how much to trust an association.
  • Genes are not the whole story: phenoconversion by co-medications matters.
  • Stable identifiers connect ClinPGx to Ensembl, UniProt, PDB and DrugBank: one biological story, many layers of information.
