From genotype to prescription with ClinPGx

A pharmacogenomics walk-through using CYP2C19 and clopidogrel as an example

Author
Affiliation

Isabel Duarte

Universidade do Algarve

Published

October 2026

Abstract

A hands-on introduction to ClinPGx (the successor of PharmGKB), the reference knowledgebase for pharmacogenomics. Following one gene (CYP2C19) and one drug (clopidogrel), you will learn the core concepts of pharmacogenomics (star alleles, diplotypes, metabolizer phenotypes) and then use ClinPGx as a hospital pharmacist would, to review the medication of a patient with a known genotype.

ImportantDisclaimer

This lesson is for education only. The genotypes and patients used here are fictional. Real prescribing decisions must follow the current version of the guidelines, the approved drug label, and the judgement of the clinical team.

1 Why is ClinPGx relevant for pharmaceutical sciences?

Two patients receive the same drug at the same dose. One responds well, the other has no benefit, and a third may have a serious adverse reaction. Part of this variability is genetic. Pharmacogenomics (PGx) studies how inherited variation in our genes changes the way we respond to medicines.

In previous classes you followed one entity at a time: a gene in Ensembl, a protein in UniProt, a structure in PDB, a drug in DrugBank. ClinPGx sits on top of all of them and answers a clinical question:

“Given this patient’s genotype, should I change the drug or the dose?”

That is a question pharmacists will increasingly be asked to answer, which is why this is our final database.

2 About ClinPGx

ClinPGx is a free resource, managed at Stanford University, that brings three projects under one roof:

The three components of ClinPGx
Project What it is What you get from it
PharmGKB The Pharmacogenomics Knowledgebase, curating PGx literature since 2000 Annotations of papers, drug labels and pathways
CPIC The Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium Peer-reviewed prescribing guidelines
PharmCAT The Pharmacogenomics Clinical Annotation Tool Software that turns a sequencing file (VCF) into a PGx report

PharmGKB was renamed ClinPGx in July 2025, and old pharmgkb.org links now redirect to the new site. In older papers you will still see the name PharmGKB: it is the same knowledgebase. One renaming to remember: what PharmGKB called clinical annotations are now called summary annotations.

3 Key concepts before we start

You only need six ideas to use ClinPGx well. Read them now; you will use each one during the session.

Note📘 Concept 1: Pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic genes
  • Pharmacokinetic (PK) genes change what the body does to the drug: absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion. Most are enzymes (e.g. the cytochromes P450, CYP2C19, CYP2D6) or transporters (e.g. SLCO1B1).
  • Pharmacodynamic (PD) genes change what the drug does to the body: the target (e.g. VKORC1 for warfarin) or the immune response (e.g. HLA-B and severe skin reactions).
Note📘 Concept 2: Star alleles and haplotypes

A gene can carry several variants at once. The combination of variants found together on one copy of a chromosome is a haplotype. In pharmacogenes, haplotypes are named with star (*) alleles: CYP2C19*1, CYP2C19*2, CYP2C19*17, and so on.

  • *1 is usually the reference (normal) allele.
  • Each other star allele is defined by one or more core variants, often identified by their dbSNP rsID (e.g. rs4244285).
  • The official naming authority for star alleles is PharmVar.
Note📘 Concept 3: Diplotype

We have two copies of each autosomal gene (one from each parent). The pair of star alleles a person carries is their diplotype, written CYP2C19*1/*2. A diplotype is the PGx equivalent of a genotype.

Note📘 Concept 4: Allele function and phenotype

Each star allele is assigned a function: normal, decreased, no function, or increased function. Combining the two alleles of a diplotype predicts a phenotype. For drug-metabolising enzymes the phenotypes are:

Standard metabolizer phenotypes
Phenotype Abbreviation Enzyme activity
Ultrarapid metabolizer UM Much higher than normal
Rapid metabolizer RM Higher than normal
Normal metabolizer NM Normal
Intermediate metabolizer IM Reduced
Poor metabolizer PM Little or none

How do two alleles give one phenotype? Think of each allele as contributing some enzyme activity, and add the two contributions together. For CYP2C19, the rules used by CPIC are:

CYP2C19: from allele functions to phenotype (simplified from CPIC)
Allele 1 Allele 2 Phenotype
increased increased Ultrarapid (UM)
normal increased Rapid (RM)
normal normal Normal (NM)
normal or increased no function Intermediate (IM)
no function no function Poor (PM)

Notice the last two rows: one no function allele is enough to reduce activity, even when the other allele has increased function. These rules are gene-specific, which is why each CPIC guideline has its own genotype-to-phenotype table.

Note📘 Concept 5: Prodrugs turn the logic upside down

For most drugs, a poor metabolizer clears the drug slowly, so drug levels rise and the risk of toxicity increases. But a prodrug is inactive until an enzyme converts it into the active form. For a prodrug, a poor metabolizer makes less active drug, so the risk is lack of efficacy.

Clopidogrel, our example today, is a prodrug activated mainly by CYP2C19.

Note📘 Concept 6: The ClinPGx knowledge pyramid

ClinPGx builds knowledge in layers, from raw literature to clinical action:

The higher you go, the more curated and the more clinically actionable the information becomes.

  3. Find the search bar, the CPIC section, and the link to the gene-drug pairs list.

  1. Which kinds of entities can you search for?
  2. Open the gene-drug pairs list (https://www.clinpgx.org/pairs). What is it built from?

  1. Genes, drugs (called chemicals), variants and haplotypes, diseases, as well as guidelines, labels, pathways and literature.
  2. It lists gene-drug pairs that have been annotated in guidelines or drug labels from CPIC, DPWG (the Dutch Pharmacogenetics Working Group) and the FDA. It is a quick way to see which pairs are clinically relevant.

5 The gene page: CYP2C19

As in UniProt, the best way to find an entry is a unique identifier, here the official HGNC gene symbol.

Tip🔬 Hands on: open the CYP2C19 page
  1. Type CYP2C19 in the search bar.
  2. Select the gene result (not a drug or a variant).
  3. Note the identifier in the URL. It starts with PA (e.g. PA124).
  4. Browse the tabs of the gene page.

Every ClinPGx object has a stable PA identifier (a legacy of PharmGKB), in the same way that UniProt has accessions like P04000 and DrugBank has identifiers like DB00758.

Warning❓ Question
  1. CYP2C19 is labelled a VIP. What does that mean?
  2. List three drugs (or drug classes) that have a CPIC guideline for CYP2C19.
  3. In the cross-references, find the identifiers for Ensembl and UniProt.
NoteShow answer
  1. Very Important Pharmacogene: a gene with strong, well documented pharmacogenomic relevance, for which ClinPGx curators write a dedicated summary.
  2. Among others: clopidogrel, proton pump inhibitors (e.g. omeprazole, pantoprazole), voriconazole, and several antidepressants (e.g. citalopram, sertraline). Keep omeprazole in mind: it comes back in the patient case.
  3. Ensembl ENSG00000165841 (chromosome 10) and UniProt P33261 (CP2CJ_HUMAN).
Tip🔗 Connection: UniProt and PDB

The UniProt entry P33261 and the PDB structure 4GQS describe the protein: a heme-containing enzyme of the endoplasmic reticulum of liver cells. ClinPGx adds the clinical layer: what happens to patients when its gene varies.

6 From alleles to phenotype

CPIC guidelines are written per phenotype, not per variant. So the single most useful skill in ClinPGx is translating a genotype into a phenotype.

6.1 Allele function

Tip🔬 Hands on: the allele function table
  1. Open the allele function pages: https://www.clinpgx.org/alleleFunctions.
  2. Select CYP2C19.
  3. Find the alleles *1, *2, *3 and *17.
Warning❓ Question
  1. What is the function assigned to *1, *2, *3 and *17?
  2. Each row of the table below is a patient, described by their two CYP2C19 alleles (their diplotype). For each patient, work in two steps:
    1. write down the function of each of the two alleles (your answer to question 1);
    2. use the rules table at the end of Concept 4 to find the phenotype that matches that pair of functions.
Patient diplotype Function of allele 1 + allele 2 Phenotype
*1/*1 normal + normal ?
*1/*17 ? ?
*17/*17 ? ?
*2/*17 ? ?
*2/*3 ? ?
NoteShow answer
  1. *1 normal function; *2 and *3 no function; *17 increased function.
  2. Phenotypes:
CYP2C19 diplotype to phenotype
Patient diplotype Function of allele 1 + allele 2 Phenotype
*1/*1 normal + normal Normal metabolizer (NM)
*1/*17 normal + increased Rapid metabolizer (RM)
*17/*17 increased + increased Ultrarapid metabolizer (UM)
*2/*17 no function + increased Intermediate metabolizer (IM)
*2/*3 no function + no function Poor metabolizer (PM)

The tricky one is *2/*17: the increased function of *17 does not compensate for the loss of *2, so CPIC classifies it as intermediate.

6.2 The variant behind CYP2C19*2

Tip🔬 Hands on: one variant, two databases
  1. In ClinPGx, search for rs4244285 and open the variant page.
  2. In a new tab, open the same variant in Ensembl: https://www.ensembl.org/Homo_sapiens/Variation/Explore?v=rs4244285.
  3. In Ensembl, look at the predicted consequence of the variant on the transcript.
Warning❓ Question
  1. Which star allele does rs4244285 define?
  2. What consequence does Ensembl predict for the protein sequence?
  3. If the amino acid does not change, how can this be a no function allele? (Hint: read the Description in the Overview section of the ClinPGx variant page.)
NoteShow answer
  1. CYP2C19*2 (c.681G>A).
  2. Ensembl reports it as a synonymous variant (the codon still encodes proline 227).
  3. The change creates an aberrant splice site. The mRNA is spliced incorrectly, the reading frame shifts, and a truncated, non-functional enzyme is produced. This is a classic lesson: “synonymous” does not mean “harmless”. Automatic predictors look at the codon; curated databases like ClinPGx integrate experimental evidence.

7 The drug page: clopidogrel

The drug page is where most clinical questions start: it gathers, in one place, the guidelines and drug labels for that drug (in the Prescribing Info area) and the summary annotations from the literature.

Tip🔬 Hands on: open the clopidogrel page
  1. Search for clopidogrel and open the chemical page.
  2. In the cross-references, find the DrugBank identifier.
  3. Locate (do not read in detail yet) the guidelines, the drug labels and the summary annotations. You will use all three in the patient case.
Warning❓ Question
  1. What is the DrugBank identifier for clopidogrel?
  2. Why is CYP2C19 so important for this particular drug? (Hint: Concept 5.)
NoteShow answer
  1. DB00758. In DrugBank you can see its target (the platelet P2Y12 receptor), its metabolising enzymes and its drug interactions.
  2. Clopidogrel is a prodrug. CYP2C19 performs most of its conversion to the active metabolite. Patients with low CYP2C19 activity make less active drug, so their platelets are less inhibited and they have a higher risk of heart attack, stroke or stent thrombosis.
Note📘 Concept 7: three scales to judge what you find

Everything on the drug page comes with a grade. You will need these three scales in the patient case, so keep this box open.

1. CPIC level of the gene-drug pair (shown in the gene-drug pairs list)

Level Meaning
A Genetic information should be used to change prescribing
B Genetic information could be used to change prescribing
C / D No prescribing action recommended

Inside a guideline, each recommendation also has a strength: strong, moderate or optional.

2. Pharmacogenomic level of a drug label (FDA, EMA, PMDA, …)

Level Meaning
Testing required Genetic testing must be done before prescribing
Testing recommended Testing is recommended
Actionable PGx The label suggests a change based on genotype, without requiring testing
Informative PGx The label mentions a gene-drug effect, with no clinical action

3. Level of evidence of a summary annotation (one variant, one drug)

Level Evidence
1A High: variant-specific guidance in a guideline or FDA label
1B High: strong, replicated association, but no guidance yet
2A / 2B Moderate
3 Low: single or conflicting studies
4 The evidence does not support an association

8 The patient case

This is the core of the lesson. You will use ClinPGx the way a hospital pharmacist would: to answer a real prescribing question, step by step.

Important🩺 The patient

Mr. A., 62 years old, has just had a coronary stent implanted (PCI) after an acute coronary syndrome. The cardiologist plans to prescribe clopidogrel (with aspirin). He has no history of stroke or major bleeding.

He has been taking omeprazole 20 mg/day for more than a year for gastric protection.

A pharmacogenomic test reports: CYP2C19 *1/*2.

You are asked to review his medication.

Warning❓ Question 1: What is his phenotype?

Translate Mr. A.’s diplotype into a CYP2C19 phenotype.

Where to look: the allele function table and the rules in Concept 4 (see Section 6.1).

NoteShow answer

*1 (normal function) + *2 (no function) = intermediate metabolizer (IM).

Warning❓ Question 2: What does the guideline recommend?

What does CPIC recommend for a CYP2C19 intermediate metabolizer who needs antiplatelet therapy after PCI? How strong is the recommendation? Would the recommendation be different if he were a poor metabolizer?

Where to look: clopidogrel page → guidelines → CPIC guideline for CYP2C19 and clopidogrel. Select the cardiovascular indication (ACS/PCI) and the phenotype.

NoteShow answer

Based on the 2022 CPIC update:

  • Intermediate metabolizer: avoid standard-dose clopidogrel if possible; use prasugrel or ticagrelor at standard dose if there is no contraindication. Strength: moderate.
  • Poor metabolizer: same advice, but the strength is strong.

Increasing the clopidogrel dose is not the solution: these patients cannot make enough active metabolite. Prasugrel and ticagrelor do not depend on CYP2C19 in the same way. Mr. A. has no history of stroke (a contraindication for prasugrel), so both alternatives are possible.

Warning❓ Question 3: How much can we trust this?
  1. What is the CPIC level of the CYP2C19-clopidogrel pair?
  2. What is the level of evidence of the summary annotation for rs4244285 and clopidogrel?
  3. Open that summary annotation and scroll to the supporting papers. Do they all agree?

Where to look: (a) the gene-drug pairs list https://www.clinpgx.org/pairs; (b, c) the summary annotations on the clopidogrel page, filtered for rs4244285. Use Concept 7 to interpret the grades.

NoteShow answer
  1. Level A: genetic information should be used to change prescribing.
  2. Level 1A: the variant has specific guidance in a guideline and a drug label, and is supported by many independent studies.
  3. No. Some studies find an association, others do not (different populations, sample sizes, outcomes). This is why ClinPGx summarises and grades many papers instead of relying on any single one, and partly why the recommendation for intermediate metabolizers is moderate rather than strong.
Warning❓ Question 4: What do the regulators say?
  1. Which agencies have a pharmacogenomic label annotation for clopidogrel?
  2. Which PGx level does ClinPGx assign to the FDA label? Is testing required?
  3. The FDA label has a boxed warning. What is it about?
  4. Does the label say the same thing as CPIC about intermediate metabolizers?

Where to look: clopidogrel page → drug labels. Use Concept 7.

NoteShow answer
  1. Several, including the FDA, EMA, PMDA, Swissmedic and Health Canada (the exact list may vary).
  2. Actionable PGx (at the time of writing). Testing is not required.
  3. Diminished antiplatelet effect in patients who are CYP2C19 poor metabolizers; the label advises considering another P2Y12 inhibitor.
  4. Not exactly. The FDA warning focuses on poor metabolizers, while CPIC also recommends alternatives for intermediate metabolizers. Labels and guidelines are written by different bodies, at different times, with different purposes. That is why ClinPGx shows both side by side.
Warning❓ Question 5: What about his omeprazole?

Mr. A.’s genotype and his omeprazole interact in two ways.

  1. Omeprazole is itself metabolised (inactivated) by CYP2C19. What does CPIC recommend for an intermediate metabolizer on long-term omeprazole?
  2. Omeprazole also inhibits CYP2C19. What does that mean for his clopidogrel? What is this effect called?
  3. Compare the effect of his genotype on clopidogrel and on omeprazole. Why does it go in opposite directions?

Where to look: (a) omeprazole page → CPIC guideline for CYP2C19 and proton pump inhibitors; (b) the clopidogrel drug labels in ClinPGx, and the clopidogrel-omeprazole interaction in DrugBank.

NoteShow answer
  1. Start with the standard dose. For chronic therapy (more than 12 weeks) with symptoms controlled, consider a 50% dose reduction and monitor efficacy. Intermediate metabolizers clear omeprazole more slowly, so they have higher exposure, which increases the risk of long-term side effects (e.g. infections, fractures).
  2. Omeprazole reduces CYP2C19 activity even further, so even less clopidogrel is activated. The FDA label advises avoiding omeprazole and esomeprazole with clopidogrel. This is phenoconversion: a co-administered drug makes the patient behave like a lower-activity phenotype than his genotype predicts (here, an intermediate metabolizer moving towards a poor metabolizer). Genes are not the whole story: always check the full medication list.
  3. Clopidogrel is a prodrug (CYP2C19 activates it): lower activity means less effect. Omeprazole is an active drug (CYP2C19 inactivates it): lower activity means more exposure. Same enzyme, same patient, opposite consequences (Concept 5).
Warning❓ Question 6: Your final recommendation
  1. Open PharmDOG, the ClinPGx prescribing tool. Enter Mr. A.’s CYP2C19 result and his two drugs (clopidogrel and omeprazole). Does the output match your answers to Questions 2 and 5? Does it warn you about the omeprazole-clopidogrel interaction?
  2. Write a short note (3 to 4 sentences) to the cardiologist with your recommendation.
NoteShow answer
  1. PharmDOG should confirm the intermediate metabolizer phenotype and the CPIC recommendations for both drugs. At the time of writing, its phenoconversion feature covered CYP2D6 only, so it may not warn you that omeprazole inhibits CYP2C19. Tools help, but the pharmacist still has to check drug-drug interactions (e.g. in DrugBank).
  2. Example note:

Mr. A. is a CYP2C19 intermediate metabolizer (*1/*2), so he is expected to activate clopidogrel poorly. According to the CPIC guideline (moderate recommendation), we suggest ticagrelor or prasugrel instead of clopidogrel, as he has no contraindication. This also avoids the clopidogrel-omeprazole interaction. Omeprazole can be continued; given his genotype and long-term use, a dose reduction could be considered if his symptoms remain controlled.

Key points

TipTake home messages
  • ClinPGx (formerly PharmGKB) integrates PharmGKB, CPIC and PharmCAT into one free pharmacogenomics resource.
  • The core reasoning is genotype → diplotype → phenotype → recommendation.
  • The drug page is the usual starting point: it gathers guidelines, drug labels and summary annotations.
  • CPIC guidelines say what to do; drug labels give the regulatory position; levels of evidence say how much to trust an association.
  • For prodrugs such as clopidogrel, low enzyme activity means less effect, not more toxicity.
  • Genes are not the whole story: co-medications can cause phenoconversion.
  • Stable identifiers connect ClinPGx to Ensembl, UniProt, PDB and DrugBank.
