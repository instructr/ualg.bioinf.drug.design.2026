Ensembl genes and transcripts | Part 2

A hands-on tour of the Ensembl browser: genes, transcripts, regions, variants, and VEP

Author

Isabel Duarte

Published

October 2026

Abstract

This is the second part of the Ensembl genome browser hands-on tutorial. You will explore a human gene, its transcripts, and the protein it encodes; export a sequence in FASTA format and search for similar sequences with BLAST; navigate the genome using coordinates; explore a single-nucleotide variant; and predict the consequences of a structural variant with the Variant Effect Predictor (VEP).

Attribution and licence

The exercises in this lesson are adapted from the Ensembl browser training material produced by EMBL-EBI and the Ensembl team, which is made available under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International licence.

Source: Ensembl training materials, https://training.ensembl.org/.

Overview

Questions this lesson answers
  • How can I find information about a gene, its transcripts, and the protein it encodes in Ensembl?
  • Where can I find the function, 3D structures, and associated diseases of a gene’s protein product?
  • How do I download a sequence from Ensembl, and what is a FASTA file?
  • How can I find sequences that are similar to my sequence of interest?
  • How do I navigate the genome using genomic coordinates?
  • How can I find information about a known genetic variant?
  • How can I predict the effects of a genetic variant on genes and transcripts?
Learning objectives

By the end of this session you should be able to:

  • Find a gene in Ensembl and use the Feature explorer to explore its transcripts and function.
  • Use the external links in Ensembl (UniProtKB, InterPro, PDBe-KB, and OMIM) to find information on protein function, 3D structure, and associated diseases.
  • Export a transcript sequence in FASTA format and describe the structure of a FASTA file.
  • Run a BLAST search to find sequences similar to a query sequence.
  • Navigate to a genomic region using coordinates, and calculate the size of a region.
  • Find a genetic variant and describe its location: gene, chromosome, coordinates, and the transcripts and transcript regions it falls in.
  • Use the Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) to predict the molecular consequences of a structural variant.
  • Critically evaluate the answers given by AI tools.
Practical details
Time estimate ~1.5 hours
Level Introductory
Prerequisites Basic knowledge of genes, transcripts, exons, UTRs, genetic variants, and phenotypes
Software needed A web browser only
Note on Ensembl updates

Ensembl publishes a new release several times a year, and the annotation changes between releases. Transcript counts, transcript names, identifier versions, and even the set of associated phenotypes may differ from the answers given here. Treat any mismatch as a discussion point rather than a mistake, and check which release you are using.

1 Exploring the human PCSK9 gene

PCSK9 encodes the proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9), a key regulator of blood cholesterol levels and the target of cholesterol-lowering drugs such as evolocumab and alirocumab.

1.4 Export the gene/transcript sequence

🔬 Hands on: export the sequence
  1. In the Feature explorer, click the transcript of interest, and then click the Download arrow.
  2. Notice that when you tick the box next to cDNA, CDS, or any of the other options, the transcript diagram highlights which parts of the sequence will be included in the downloaded file.
  3. Select cDNA and click Download.
  4. Save the file and open it to look at its contents. This file is in FASTA format.
  5. Go to the AI search engine Perplexity and ask it what a FASTA file is, using this prompt: “In the context of bioinformatics, what is a FASTA file? Explain it using a clear, scientific tone, for postgraduate students.”
  6. Read the answer and think critically: does what it tells you make sense?
  7. Compare your answer with those of the classmates at the computers next to you.
❓ Question

What information does the FASTA header contain?

Show answer

A FASTA header is the first line of each sequence entry, and it starts with a > symbol. It describes the sequence that begins on the next line. The header should include a stable and unique identifier for the sequence, and it often includes other details, such as the gene name and the species. For example:

>ENST00000302118.5|PCSK9|Homo_sapiens

2 Using BLAST to find similar sequences

Are there other similar genes (homologues) in the human genome? To answer this, we can use BLAST (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool) to search a sequence database for sequences that are similar to our sequence of interest.

🔬 Hands on: BLAST app
  1. Click the BLAST icon at the top of the page (the lightning bolt).
  2. Click the help button (?) and read the instructions on how to run a BLAST search.
  3. Paste the FASTA sequence you downloaded in the previous exercise, or drag and drop the file to upload it.
  4. Keep the default parameters and click Run.
  5. Wait until the job is complete, and then click Results.

4 Exploring a genetic variant

The SNP rs1738074 has been reported as a genetic risk factor for several diseases. Now we want to explore in detail what this variant is, and why it might be a genetic risk factor.

💡 Remember: what are genetic variants?

A genetic variant is a difference in the DNA sequence between individuals, or between an individual and the reference genome. Variants range in size from a single base (single-nucleotide variants, SNVs; common SNVs are often called SNPs) to small insertions and deletions, up to large structural variants that can delete or duplicate entire genes.

Why do they matter in pharmaceutical sciences?

  • Drug response (pharmacogenomics): variants in genes that encode drug-metabolising enzymes, such as CYP2D6 or CYP2C19, change how patients respond to a drug, affecting both its efficacy and the risk of adverse reactions.
  • Drug targets: people who carry natural loss-of-function variants in PCSK9 have low LDL cholesterol and a lower risk of heart disease. This finding helped establish PCSK9 as a drug target.
  • Diagnosis and treatment choice: some variants increase disease risk, and others, such as those in BRCA2, can guide diagnosis and treatment decisions.
🔬 Hands on: find the variant
  1. Search Ensembl directly for rs1738074.
  2. Open the Genome browser view and inspect the information about this genetic variant.
🔎 Want to know more about this variant?

Click the ... (three dots) in the top-right corner of the variant tab, next to the name rs1738074. This gives you access to more information about the variant, including its population genetics data. We will not explore this further in this class, but it is useful to know that the linked dbSNP database provides detailed information on genetic variants, such as their associations with phenotypes and diseases, in case you ever need to explore them further.

5 VEP analysis of a structural variant

A tumour sample from a patient with breast cancer carries a large genomic deletion, described here in VCF (Variant Call Format):

13  32307062    sv1 .   <DEL>   .   .   SVTYPE=DEL;END=32908738

In plain words, this line describes a deletion (<DEL>) on chromosome 13 that starts at position 32,307,062 and ends at position 32,908,738 (about 600 kb).

We now want to know whether this variant could be relevant to the disease: which genes does it affect, and how? To answer this, we will use the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP).

💡 What is VEP?

VEP is a tool that takes a list of genetic variants (for example, from a patient’s sequencing data) and predicts their effects. For each variant, VEP reports:

  • the genes and transcripts that it overlaps;
  • where in the transcript it falls (for example, in an exon, an intron, or a UTR);
  • its predicted molecular consequence, using standard terms (for example, missense_variant when one amino acid is replaced by another, or transcript_ablation when a whole transcript is deleted).

Why is this important? Sequencing a person’s genome typically reveals millions of variants, and most of them are harmless. VEP is a first step to sort through them and find the few that are likely to affect gene function, and that could therefore help with a diagnosis or a treatment decision. Keep in mind that these are predictions: they must be confirmed by experimental or clinical evidence.

🔬 Hands on: run VEP
  1. Click Ensembl VEP at the top of any Ensembl page.
  2. Read the help page (click the ? sign).
  3. Add the Human genome (click the + sign in the Genome row).
  4. Give the job a descriptive name, for example Patient deletion exercise. Naming jobs is a good habit: the name is how you tell results apart later, and it is what appears in the browser when you view the data.
  5. Click the + sign in the Variants row, and paste the VCF line shown above (use the copy button in the top-right corner of the code block).
  6. Click Add.
  7. Keep the default job options and click Run. By default, the results list the genes and transcripts that each variant falls in, and the predicted molecular consequence for each one.
❓ Question
  1. How many genes are affected?
  2. What are the predicted molecular consequences of this variant?
Show answer
  1. 7 genes are affected: N4BP2L2, PDS5B, N4BP2L1, BRCA2, ZAR1L, IFIT1P1, and ATP8A2P2. Notice that BRCA2, a well-known breast cancer susceptibility gene, is among them.
  2. Mostly transcript_ablation, but also coding_sequence_variant, 5_prime_UTR_variant, and intron_variant for the ZAR1L gene.

Critical thinking exercise: Use the AI chatbot Perplexity to learn the meaning of the most common predicted molecular consequence. Ask: “In the context of the Variant Effect Predictor for genomic variant analysis, what does ‘[insert the most common consequence here]’ mean? Explain it using a clear, concise, scientific yet simple tone, for postgraduate students.” As before, check critically whether the answer makes sense.

Key points

Take-home messages
  • You can search Ensembl using a gene name, a variant identifier (such as an rs number), or a set of genomic coordinates.
  • Ensembl links to specialised databases (UniProtKB, InterPro, PDBe-KB, OMIM, and dbSNP) with detailed information on protein function, structure, diseases, and variants.
  • A gene usually has many transcripts. Choose one deliberately, and prefer the MANE Select transcript when available (a representative transcript agreed on by Ensembl and NCBI).
  • Genomic coordinates are only meaningful with an assembly version (for example, GRCh38), and alleles are only meaningful with a strand.
  • VEP predicts the consequences of variants of any size, from single-nucleotide variants to large structural variants, and helps you find the ones most likely to matter.
  • AI tools can help you learn, but always check their answers critically.
