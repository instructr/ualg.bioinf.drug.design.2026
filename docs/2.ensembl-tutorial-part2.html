Ensembl genes and transcripts | Part 2

A hands-on tour of the Ensembl browser: genes, transcripts, regions, variants, and VEP

Author

Isabel Duarte

Published

23 September 2026

Abstract

Second part of five short practicals on the Ensembl genome browser. You will explore a human gene and its transcripts, navigate and configure a genomic region, interpret population and phenotype data for a SNP, and predict the consequences of a structural variant with the Variant Effect Predictor.

ImportantAttribution and licence

The exercises in this lesson are adapted from the Ensembl browser training material produced by EMBL-EBI and the Ensembl team, which is made available under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International licence.

Source: Ensembl training materials, https://training.ensembl.org/.

Overview

NoteQuestions this lesson answers
  • Where do phenotype, disease, and Gene Ontology annotations appear, and where do they come from?
  • How do you inspect exons, untranslated regions, cross-references, and expression probes for a single transcript?
  • How do you start from a disease and work back to a gene?
  • How do you navigate, configure, and export a genomic region?
  • How do you read allele frequencies, ancestral alleles, and conservation for a SNP?
  • How do you predict the consequences of a structural variant with VEP?
TipLearning objectives

By the end of this session you should be able to:

  1. Search Ensembl by phenotype, and by genomic coordinates.
  2. Export sequences in FASTA format.
  3. Interpret population genetics, conservation, and phenotype association data for a variant.
  4. Run the Variant Effect Predictor on a structural variant and filter the results.
Practical details
Time estimate ~1.5 hours
Level Introductory
Prerequisites Genes, transcripts, exons, UTRs, variants, and phenotype
Software needed A web browser only
CautionNote on Ensembl updates

Ensembl publishes a new release several times a year, and annotation changes between releases. Transcript counts, transcript names, identifier versions, and even the set of associated phenotypes may differ from the answers given here. Treat any mismatch as a discussion point rather than a mistake: look at which release you are looking at.

1 Exploring the PCSK9 gene in human

PCSK9 encodes the proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9.

1.1 Navigate to the human PCSK9 gene

Use the skills acquired last week to search again for the gene of interest in Ensembl:

1.2 Feature explorer

Explore the transcripts, the function, and the homology for our gene of interest in the Feature explorer app.

Tip🔬 Hands on
  1. Click on the Feature explorer icon on top.
  2. Confirm that the correct gene of interest is selected and being shown.
  3. Click the Gene function tab on top.
  4. Follow the three external links: UniprotKB, Interpro and PDBe-KB, that allow you to learn more about this gene and its protein.
  5. The UniprotKB shows information on the protein.
  6. Interpro shows information on the protein domains.
  7. PDBe-KB shows information on the protein 3D structure.
Warning❓ Question
  1. How many known structures are available for the protein encoded by this gene?
  2. How many ligands does it have? Can you name three of them? Where did you get this information?
  3. What functions is PCSK9 annotated with? Where did you get this information?

  3. The function is presented in all 3 databases (UniprotKB, Interpro, and PDBe-KB) :

Binds to low-density lipid receptor family members: low density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR), very low density lipoprotein receptor (VLDLR), apolipoprotein E receptor (LRP1/APOER) and apolipoprotein receptor 2 (LRP8/APOER2), and promotes their degradation in intracellular acidic compartments.

1.4 Export the gene/transcript sequence

Tip🔬 Hands on: export the sequence
  1. In the Feature explorer, click the transcript of interest, and next click the Download arrow.
  2. Notice that when you select the box next to cDNA or CDS, or any of the options, the cartoon gene highlights which parts of the sequence will be present in the download file.
  3. Select cDNA and click Download.
  4. Save the file and open it to look at it. This file format is called: FASTA format.
  5. Navigate to the AI search engine provider Perplexity, and query it to learn about what a FASTA format is by asking: “In the context of bioinformatics, what is a FASTA file? Explain it using clear, scientific tone, for postgradute students.”
  6. Read the answer and use your critical thought to check if what it is telling you makes sense.
  7. Compare your answer to the answers from your neighbouring computer classemates.
Warning❓ Question

What information does the FASTA header carry?

NoteShow answer

A FASTA header starts with a > symbol, and it provides information related to the sequence starting in th next line. This information should include a stable and unique identifier. For example:

>ENST00000335137.4|TP53|Homo_sapiens

3 Exploring a genomic region

This exercise moves from gene-centric to coordinate-centric browsing.

Tip🔬 Hands on: navigate to the region

  1. Go to the genome browser view of the PCSK9 gene.

  2. Type the following coordinates in the search box and click Go:

    Y:1-57227415

  3. Notice that you are seeing the complete human reference Y chromosome.

  4. Zoom in (using the plus sign or the mouse wheel) and scroll horizontally to choose a region that is enriched with protein coding genes.

  5. Briefly explore the genes present in that region, their sizes, and their annotated functions.

Warning❓ Question
  1. What is the size of the whole Y chromosome? (Hint: The size of a genomic region calculated from an interval is: final coordinate - start coordinate + 1)
  2. How many coding genes are in your selected region?
NoteShow answer
  1. 57227415 (In this case, the size is the same as the final coordinate of the genomic region.)
  2. I chose the following coordinates: Y:1084826-6714927. Here, there are 10 genes in the forward strand, and 7 genes in the reverse strand. I just counted them by eye (since this new Ensembl interface is not yet fully developed to explore a genomic region.)

5 VEP analysis of a structural variant

A breast cancer sample from a patient presents a genomic deletion, described here in VCF format:

13  32307062    sv1 .   <DEL>   .   .   SVTYPE=DEL;END=32908738

We now want to know if this variant is relevant for the disease… [Explain what is VEP and why it is important for students: clear, concise, simple, pedagogical and informative.]

Tip🔬 Hands on: run VEP
  1. Click Ensebl VEP at the top of any Ensembl page.
  2. Read the help page (click the ? sign).
  3. Add the Human genome (+ sign in the Genome row).
  4. Give the job a descriptive name, for example Patient deletion exercise. Naming jobs is a good habit: it is how you tell results apart later, and the name is what appears in the browser when you view the data.
  5. Click the + sign in the variant row, and paste the text 13 32307062 sv1 . <DEL> . . SVTYPE=DEL;END=32908738.
  6. Click Add.
  7. Leave the default job options (by default, the results will name the genes and transcripts the variants fall in, and report the predicted molecular consequence for each), and click Run.
Warning❓ Question
  1. How many genes are affected?
  2. What are the predicted molecular consequences of this variant?
NoteShow answer
  1. 7 genes are affected: N4BP2L2, PDS5B, N4BP2L1, BRCA2, ZAR1L, IFIT1P1, and ATP8A2P2.
  2. Mostly transcript ablation, but also coding_sequence_variant, 5_prime_UTR_variant, and intron_variant for the ZAR1L gene.

Critical thought exercise: Use the AI chatbot Perplexity to learn what is the meaning of the most common predicted molecular consequence: “In the context of variant effect predictor for genomic variant analysis, what is the meaning of”[insert most common value here]“? Explain using clear, concise, scientific yet simple tone for postgraduate students.”

Key points

TipTake home messages
  • Ensembl can be entered from a gene name, or a set of coordinates.
  • A gene usually has many transcripts. Pick one deliberately, and prefer MANE
  • Genomic coordinates mean nothing without an assembly version, and alleles mean nothing without a strand.
  • VEP scales the same reasoning up from single bases to structural variants, and its consequence filters are how you find the events that matter.
