One drug along the DrugBank page

Exploring a DrugBank entry using atorvastatin as an example

Author
Affiliation

Isabel Duarte

Universidade do Algarve

Published

October 2026

Abstract

A hands-on walk through a single DrugBank entry (DB01076, atorvastatin). You will learn how to find a drug, read its mechanism, pharmacokinetics, interactions and properties, and follow its links to UniProt and the PDB.

Without an account, DrugBank shows only a short summary of each entry. Please create a free account before the session, so we can start on time.

  1. Go to https://go.drugbank.com/public_users/sign_up.
  2. Register and confirm the email you receive.
  3. Log in once to check that it works.

Overview

NoteQuestions this lesson answers
  • How do you find a drug in DrugBank quickly?
  • How are a drug’s mechanism, targets and pharmacokinetics described?
  • How can DrugBank help you anticipate interactions and differences between patients?
  • How do you move from a drug to its target protein and to its 3D structure?
TipLearning objectives

By the end of this session you should be able to:

  1. Interpret a DrugBank entry: mechanism, targets, pharmacokinetics, interactions and classification.
  2. Explain a clinically relevant interaction using the enzymes and transporters listed for a drug.
  3. Use shared identifiers (UniProt accession, PDB ID) to move between databases.

1 Why is DrugBank relevant for pharmaceutical sciences?

In the UniProt session you started from a protein. Today you start from the molecule that acts on it.

DrugBank gathers in one entry what a pharmacist or drug developer needs to know about a drug: its chemistry, what it binds, how the body absorbs, transforms and eliminates it, what it interacts with, and how it is marketed. Each piece links to other databases (UniProt, PubChem, ChEMBL, the PDB) through shared identifiers.

2 About this lesson

This lesson uses atorvastatin (Lipitor) as a worked example. Atorvastatin is a statin: it lowers blood cholesterol and reduces the risk of heart attack and stroke. For many years it was the best-selling medicine in the world, and it is still one of the most prescribed.

3 Finding the entry

Tip🔬 Hands on: find atorvastatin
  1. Open https://go.drugbank.com and log in.
  2. Search for atorvastatin and open the first hit (or go directly to https://go.drugbank.com/drugs/DB01076).
Warning❓ Question
  1. What is the DrugBank accession number of atorvastatin?
  2. Search for the brand name Lipitor. Where do you land?
NoteShow answer
  1. DB01076. Every DrugBank drug has a stable accession: DB + 5 digits.
  2. On the same entry. DrugBank indexes brand names and synonyms, but the entry is always the active substance, not a commercial product.

4 Reading the entry page

As in UniProt, the left-hand menu stays fixed as you scroll and shows what the entry holds. We will follow it from top to bottom:

  • Identification
  • Pharmacology
  • Interactions
  • Products
  • Categories
  • Chemical Identifiers
  • References
  • Clinical Trials
  • Pharmacoeconomics
  • Properties
  • Spectra
  • Targets, Enzymes
  • Carriers, Transporters

We will skip Clinical Trials, Pharmacoeconomics and Spectra today.

4.1 The summary card

At the top of the page is the “ID card” of the drug: structure, description, DrugBank ID, modality, number of indicated conditions, clinical trials by phase, therapeutic categories and mechanism of action.

Warning❓ Question
  1. What is the modality of atorvastatin?
  2. How many clinical trials are listed in Phase 4? Why is an approved drug still being tested?
  3. In the Identification section, which Groups is atorvastatin in?
NoteShow answer
  1. Small molecule (as opposed to, for example, an antibody).
    1. Phase 4 trials take place after approval: they monitor long-term safety, compare treatments, or test new uses.
  3. Approved and Investigational: it is marketed, and still under study.

4.2 Identification

Read the Background paragraph.

Warning❓ Question
  1. When was atorvastatin first synthesised, and when was it approved by the FDA?
  2. Unlike some other statins, atorvastatin does not need activation. What does that mean?
NoteShow answer
  1. Synthesised in 1985, approved by the FDA in 1996.
  2. It is given in its active form. Some statins (for example lovastatin and simvastatin) are prodrugs that must be converted in the body before they can inhibit the enzyme.

4.3 Pharmacology

This is the core of the entry. Read Mechanism of action and the small target table below it.

Warning❓ Question
  1. Which enzyme does atorvastatin inhibit, and which reaction does that enzyme catalyse?
  2. Atorvastatin blocks cholesterol synthesis in the liver. Why does that lower cholesterol in the blood?
  3. The target table lists five proteins, each with a letter badge, A or U. Which one has an A, and what do you think the letters mean? (Hint: Look at the Targets cards at the bottom of the page and compare this table with its Pharmacological action field.)
NoteShow answer
  1. Competitive inhibitor of HMG-CoA (3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A) reductase. It converts HMG-CoA to mevalonate, the rate-limiting step of cholesterol synthesis.
  2. Liver cells with less cholesterol produce more LDL receptors, which pull LDL (sometimes called the “bad cholesterol”) out of the blood.
  3. Only HMG-CoA reductase has an A: binding to it is known to cause the drug’s effect (Pharmacological Action: yes). The other four (DPP4, AHR, HDAC2, NR1I3) have U: the drug binds them, but whether that matters is Unknown.

Now read carefully in the Pharmacology section the fields related to pharmacokinetic characteristics of the drug:

  • Absorption,
  • Protein binding,
  • Metabolism,
  • Route of elimination and
  • Half-life.
Warning❓ Question
  1. What is the absolute oral bioavailability, and why is it so low?
  2. Which enzyme is mainly responsible for the metabolism? Are the metabolites active? (Look at the metabolite tree.)
  3. The half-life of atorvastatin is 14 hours, yet the effect lasts longer. Why?
  4. How is atorvastatin mainly eliminated?
NoteShow answer
  1. 14%, because of extensive first-pass metabolism in the gut wall and liver.
  2. Cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4 gene), producing ortho- and para-hydroxylated metabolites. Yes, the metabolites are active: they account for about 70% of the circulating HMG-CoA reductase inhibition.
  3. The active metabolites have half-lives of up to 30 hours.
  4. In the bile. Less than 1% is eliminated by the kidneys.

Genes can change how a patient handles a drug. Re-read the end of the Absorption section, then scroll to the Pharmacogenomic Effects/ADRs table.

Warning❓ Question
  1. Patients homozygous for the SLCO1B1 c.521T>C variant have a 2.45-fold higher atorvastatin AUC. SLCO1B1 gene encodes OATP1B1 protein, which carries the drug from the blood into liver cells. Why would these patients be at greater risk of muscle toxicity? (Hint: See the Myopathy/Rhabdomyolysis description in the Pharmacodynamics section.)
  2. Which three genes are listed in the Pharmacogenomic Effects/ADRs table? Do they change the drug’s efficacy or its safety?
NoteShow answer
  1. Less uptake into the liver means more drug stays in the blood and reaches muscle, where statins can cause myopathy (see Pharmacodynamics). The higher dose in the wrong tissue is the problem, not a stronger effect.
  2. HMGCR and CYP3A4 (CYP3A4*1B)describe efficacy: a greater or smaller reduction in LDL cholesterol; while KIF6 describes a safety related finding (reduced risk of major cardiovascular event).
Warning❓ Question

Critical thinking question

Ask your favourite Large Language Model (LLM) AI chat:

“What is the half-life of atorvastatin, which enzyme metabolises it, and what is your source? Answer briefly.”

Compare the answer with DrugBank. Does it match? Does the source exist and say what the model claims? Which would you cite in a report, and why?

NoteShow answer

LLMs usually get well-known facts like these right, but they can mix up values (for example, the half-life of the drug and of its metabolites), and the references they give may be inaccurate or invented. DrugBank attaches a numbered reference to each statement, which you can open and check.

4.4 Interactions

Tip🔬 Hands on
  1. In Drug Interactions, note the number of entries under the default APPROVED tab, then click ALL DRUGS.
  2. Type clarithromycin in the table’s search box.
  3. Read the Food Interactions.
Warning❓ Question
  1. How many interactions with approved drugs are listed? Why does the number grow under ALL DRUGS?
  2. What does DrugBank say about clarithromycin? Explain it using what you learnt in the Pharmacology section.
  3. Can Atorvastatin be taken with food? Which food should patients avoid, and why?
NoteShow answer
    1. ALL DRUGS () also includes investigational, experimental, nutraceutical and other substances.
  2. “The risk or severity of rhabdomyolysis can be increased when Clarithromycin is combined with Atorvastatin.” This is because Clarithromycin decreases the metabolism of atorvastatin. It is a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor, so atorvastatin levels rise, and with them the risk of myopathy and rhabdomyolysis.
  3. Atorvastatin can be taken with or without food. Grapefruit should be avoided because it increases the risk of myopathy (since it inhibits CYP3A4 in the gut wall).

4.5 Products and Categories

Warning❓ Question
  1. In Products, look at International/Other Brands. Under which brand name is atorvastatin sold in Portugal? By which pharmaceutical company?
  2. How many generic prescription products are listed? Can you propose why so many?
  3. Look at Unapproved/Other Products. Does anything surprise you? In which country are they sold?
  4. In Categories, decode the ATC code C10AA05 level by level. The other ATC codes are combination products: with which kinds of drugs is atorvastatin combined?
NoteShow answer
  1. Sortis is the brand name in Portugal, sold by Pfizer.
  2. 909 generics. Since the patent has expired, many companies sell generics, in several strengths and pack sizes.
  3. Two oral liquids, including a “coffee”, list atorvastatin as an ingredient. A database faithfully records what has been registered somewhere, not only what makes medical sense. Always check the source.
  4. C cardiovascular system › C10 lipid modifying agents › C10A lipid modifying agents, plain › C10AA HMG CoA reductase inhibitors › C10AA05 atorvastatin. The combinations add other cardiovascular drugs: ezetimibe (another lipid-lowering drug), antihypertensives (amlodipine, perindopril, ramipril, candesartan) and acetylsalicylic acid. Several of them are “polypills” for cardiovascular prevention.

4.6 Chemical Identifiers and Properties

The Chemical Identifiers section provides standardized names and machine-readable representations that uniquely identify a drug’s chemical structure and allow it to be linked across databases.

Identifier What it is Typical use
UNII FDA Unique Ingredient Identifier Standardized identification of substances in FDA-related data.
CAS Registry Number A unique numeric identifier assigned by Chemical Abstracts Service Searching and matching chemicals across regulatory and chemical databases.
InChIKey Fixed 27-character hash derived from InChI Compact, searchable identifier for web and database searches.
InChI IUPAC International Chemical Identifier; a standardized structural text identifier Precise structure-based database linking.
IUPAC name Systematic chemical name following IUPAC rules Unambiguous human-readable description of the molecule.
SMILES Text string encoding the 2D molecular structure Input for cheminformatics tools, structure drawing, and computational analysis.

SMILES and InChI describe the structure, while CAS, UNII, and DrugBank IDs are database identifiers used to retrieve and cross-reference the same compound. DrugBank also provides structure downloads in formats such as SDF, SMILES, and InChI for computational workflows.

The Properties lists experimental and predicted physicochemical values.

Note | A classic first check in drug design is Lipinski’s Rule of Five (Ro5): a rule of thumb in medicinal chemistry used to estimate whether a small molecule is likely to be orally bioavailable in humans (i.e., absorbed well enough after ingestion to reach its target). It was introduced by Christopher Lipinski in 1997 and is a screening guideline, not proof that a compound will be an effective drug.

According to this rule, an active drug usually has no more than one violation of these rules:

Property Preferred limit Why it matters
Molecular weight ≤ 500 Da Very large molecules often have poor membrane permeability
Calculated log P (ClogP) ≤ 5 Measures lipophilicity; too high can reduce solubility and increase nonspecific binding
Hydrogen-bond donors ≤ 5 Mainly OH and NH groups; too many can impede passage through membranes
Hydrogen-bond acceptors ≤ 10 Mainly O and N atoms; excessive polarity can reduce permeability
Warning❓ Question
  1. In the Properties section, sub-section Predicted Properties, what does DrugBank say about the Rule of Five? Using the molecular weight (shown in the Identification section), logP, donor and acceptor counts, which rules are broken?
  2. You will find three different logP values on the Properties section. Why do they differ?
  3. Atorvastatin fails the Rule of Five, yet it is one of the most used oral drugs. What does that tell you about such rules?
NoteShow answer
  1. Rule of Five: No. Molecular weight 558.6 > 500, and logP 5.39 (Chemaxon) > 5. Donors (4) and Acceptors (5) are fine.
  2. One is experimental (6.36) and two are predicted by different programs (ALOGPS 4.24, Chemaxon 5.39). Predictions depend on the method; always check which value you are using.
  3. They are quick filters, not laws. Atorvastatin is well absorbed (the predicted ADMET table also says so); its low bioavailability comes from first-pass metabolism, which these rules do not capture.

4.7 Targets, Enzymes, Carriers and Transporters

The last part of the page has one card per protein associated with this drug (targets, metabolizing enzymes, carriers, and transporters), each with its UniProt ID, Pharmacological action and Actions (inhibitor, substrate, inducer, binder…).

Warning❓ Question
  1. How many enzymes, carriers and transporters are listed? (Hint: Look at the left-hand menu.)
  2. Which carrier is listed? How does it relate to the Protein binding percentage you read earlier in the Pharmacology section?
  3. In the HMG-CoA reductase card, click the UniProt ID P04035. What is the UniProt entry name, and where is the protein located in the cell?
NoteShow answer
  1. 10 enzymes, 1 carrier, 10 transporters.
  2. Albumin (action: binder). Since the carrier protein is albumin, the drug is transported in the blood bound to albumin, which explains why more than 98% of atorvastatin in plasma is protein-bound.
  3. The protein name in UniProt is HMDH_HUMAN. It is a membrane protein located in the endoplasmic reticulum and in the peroxisome. Note that the DrugBank and UniProt databases are linked by the shared accession number P04035.

5 From drug to structure

DrugBank tells you that atorvastatin binds HMG-CoA reductase. The Protein Data Bank (PDB) shows you how, atom by atom.

Tip🔬 Hands on
  1. In DrugBank, copy the InChIKey of atorvastatin (Chemical Identifiers).
  2. Go to References › External Links. Note the PDBe Ligand ID and click the entry under PDB Entries (1hwk).
  3. You are now on the RCSB PDB page. Scroll to Small Molecules.
Warning❓ Question
  1. Compare the InChIKey of the ligand with the one you copied (Hint: You can use CTRL + f to search for the InChIKey). What do you conclude?
  2. Which experimental method was used, and at what resolution?
  3. The protein in this structure has four chains. How many copies of atorvastatin are bound? (Hint: You can hide the Polymer and the Water in the right-hand side menu, and keep visible only the Ligands. If you hover the mouse over the ligands you can see their name in the bottom right corner (id 117)).
NoteShow answer
  1. In the PDB entry 1HWK, ligand 117 has the same InChIKey (XUKUURHRXDUEBC-KAYWLYCHSA-N) as atorvastatin in the DrugBank entry, so ligand 117 in PDB is atorvastatin, even though the two databases use different identifiers for it.
  2. X-ray diffraction, 2.22 Å (Istvan and Deisenhofer, Science, 2001).
  3. Four, one in each chain.

Key points

TipTake home messages
  • A DrugBank entry gathers chemistry, mechanism, pharmacokinetics, interactions and products of a drug in one place.
  • Targets explain the effect; enzymes and transporters explain most interactions and much of the variability between patients.
  • Not every listed target matters: check the pharmacological action.
  • Predictions (logP, Rule of Five, ADMET) are useful filters, not facts.
  • Shared identifiers (UniProt ID, InChIKey, PDB ID) connect drug, protein and structure databases.
